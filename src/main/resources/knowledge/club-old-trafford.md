Old Trafford has been Manchester United's home since February 1910. With a capacity of roughly 74,000 it is the largest club stadium in the United Kingdom (only the national stadium, Wembley, is bigger), nicknamed the "Theatre of Dreams" by Sir Bobby Charlton. In March 2025 the club announced plans for a new ~100,000-seat stadium on adjacent land.

Old Trafford was built with brewery owner John Henry Davies's money and designed by Archibald Leitch, the era's leading football architect. The first match, on 19 February 1910, ended in a 4-3 defeat to Liverpool. The stadium's record attendance — 76,962 — was set at an FA Cup semi-final between Wolves and Grimsby in March 1939.

On 11 March 1941, German bombs aimed at the neighbouring Trafford Park industrial estate destroyed much of Old Trafford, including the main stand. Manchester United played home games at Manchester City's Maine Road until Old Trafford reopened in August 1949.

Post-war rebuilding proceeded in stages: floodlights in 1957, the cantilevered United Road stand for the 1966 World Cup (Old Trafford hosted matches at the 1966 World Cup and Euro 96), conversion to all-seater in the early 1990s after the Taylor Report, and major expansions — the North Stand's three tiers (1996) and the quadrant fillings completed in 2006 that took capacity to about 76,000 (later reduced to ~74,300 by seating changes).

Landmarks around the ground carry the club's memory. The Munich Clock shows 6 February 1958, the date of the Munich air disaster; the Munich Tunnel memorial sits beneath the South Stand. The Holy Trinity statue (2008) of George Best, Denis Law and Bobby Charlton faces a statue of Sir Matt Busby on Sir Matt Busby Way; a statue of Sir Alex Ferguson stands before the stand renamed for him in 2011. The South Stand was renamed the Sir Bobby Charlton Stand in 2016.

By the 2020s Old Trafford had visibly decayed — a leaking roof became a symbol of the Glazer era's under-investment. After Sir Jim Ratcliffe's INEOS took control of football operations in February 2024, a task force examined redevelopment, and in March 2025 the club announced its intention to build a new 100,000-seat stadium adjacent to the current site, an estimated £2 billion project — set to become the biggest sporting arena in the UK.

The new-stadium project advanced substantially in 2026. Manchester United secured the majority of the land required, acquiring a 25-acre site approximately 350 metres north-west of the current Old Trafford stadium from Indurent, an industrial-space provider and Blackstone portfolio company. The proposed stadium will anchor a purpose-built "Stadium District" at the heart of the Trafford Wharfside masterplan, published by Trafford Council and supported by the Old Trafford Regeneration Mayoral Development Corporation (OTR MDC), the Greater Manchester Combined Authority (GMCA) and Transport for Greater Manchester (TfGM). Collette Roche leads the project as CEO of Manchester United's New Stadium Development, emphasising that the club is building the stadium "with our supporters, not just for them", with atmosphere, affordability and accessibility as stated priorities, and that building beside Old Trafford preserves matchday heritage, traditions and rituals.

The wider Trafford Wharfside regeneration — around 370 acres (150 hectares) of mixed-use development — is projected to deliver roughly 15,000 new homes including affordable housing, create 48,000 local jobs and over 90,000 nationally, and add more than £7 billion per year to the UK economy.

Financing and design remained open questions as the project moved forward. Manchester United carried about £1.3 billion of debt as of July 2026, and while CEO Omar Berrada had stated in March 2025 that £2 billion was the stadium's working cost estimate, Collette Roche (CEO of the New Stadium Development) declined in July 2026 to confirm that figure still held, citing the stadium's unprecedented scale (100,000 seats, larger than any other in the UK) and stating that debt, equity, shares and outside investors all remained funding options, alongside potential revenue from selling the stadium's naming rights. Roche framed the project as "a sanity, not vanity project," pointing to the on-time, on-budget £50 million Carrington training-ground redevelopment as evidence of financial discipline under INEOS, which has also cut around 450 jobs at the club since Sir Jim Ratcliffe's investment. The initially unveiled design drew fan criticism for resembling a "circus tent"; Roche said the design was not final. Process-wise, Trafford Council's executive committee was scheduled to decide on 20 July 2026 whether to approve the draft Wharfside strategic masterplan, with an eight-week public consultation to follow from 28 July 2026 if approved.
