TOPIC: Manchester United ownership — Davies to the Glazers and INEOS
CATEGORY: club   AS OF: 2026-07   STATUS: stable-history (verified July 2026)

Manchester United's ownership story runs from brewery money (John Henry Davies, 1902) through local industrialists to a stock-market flotation (1991), the Glazer family's debt-loaded takeover (2005), and Sir Jim Ratcliffe's INEOS buying a minority stake with control of football operations (February 2024). Ownership has been the club's most persistent fan grievance of the modern era.

John Henry Davies rescued the bankrupt Newton Heath in 1902, renamed it Manchester United and funded Old Trafford. James W. Gibson saved the club from a second bankruptcy in 1931 and backed the youth system. The Edwards family — Louis Edwards, then his son Martin Edwards — controlled the club from the 1960s; Martin Edwards floated Manchester United on the London Stock Exchange in 1991. In 1999 the UK government blocked Rupert Murdoch's BSkyB from buying Manchester United on competition grounds. In the early 2000s Irish racing magnates John Magnier and J.P. McManus became the largest shareholders and feuded with Alex Ferguson over the racehorse Rock of Gibraltar — a saga that opened the door for the Glazers, who bought the Irish investors' stake.

Malcolm Glazer, an American businessman who owned the NFL's Tampa Bay Buccaneers, completed a hostile leveraged buyout of Manchester United in May 2005, valuing the club at about £790 million. Crucially, the takeover loaded the club itself with the acquisition debt — Manchester United has since paid out well over £1 billion in interest, fees and dividends. Fan fury was immediate: some supporters founded the breakaway club FC United of Manchester in 2005, and in January 2010 the green-and-gold protest movement filled Old Trafford with Newton Heath's original colours. Malcolm Glazer died in 2014; his six children retained control, with Joel Glazer and Avram Glazer as co-chairmen. The Glazers listed Manchester United on the New York Stock Exchange in August 2012 (ticker: MANU).

Protests intensified after the Glazers signed Manchester United up to the failed European Super League in April 2021 — fans invaded Old Trafford and forced the postponement of a match against Liverpool. In November 2022 the Glazers announced a "strategic review", inviting bids. After a drawn-out process contested by Qatari banker Sheikh Jassim, Sir Jim Ratcliffe — founder of the chemicals giant INEOS and a boyhood Manchester United fan — completed the purchase of an initial ~27.7% minority stake in February 2024 (later increased to just under 29%), with an agreement handing INEOS control of football operations while the Glazers remain majority owners.

INEOS's tenure began with sweeping cost-cutting (including significant staff redundancies), executive churn (Dan Ashworth arrived as sporting director in 2024 and left within months), the hiring of Ruben Amorim as head coach in November 2024, and the March 2025 announcement of a planned new ~100,000-seat stadium. Ruben Amorim was sacked in January 2026 and replaced by Michael Carrick, first as short-term head coach (announced 13 January 2026) and then permanently from 22 May 2026 on a contract to 2028.

As of July 2026, Sir Jim Ratcliffe's INEOS holds approximately a 29% stake in Manchester United — unchanged since the 2024 purchase and subsequent top-up — while the Glazer family retains majority ownership and sits on the corporate board alongside INEOS representatives. Within INEOS Sport, Sir Dave Brailsford serves as Director and Jean-Claude Blanc as CEO of INEOS Sport, shaping the football division's overarching strategy.

Manchester United's executive structure as of 2026: Omar Berrada is Chief Executive Officer, overseeing all day-to-day business and football operations; Collette Roche is Chief Operating Officer, handling operational logistics including the Carrington training centre (Collette Roche also serves as CEO of the New Stadium Development); Jason Wilcox is Director of Football, working directly beneath the CEO and directing football strategy; and Christopher Vivell is Head of Recruitment, focusing on scouting, data-driven player filtering and transfer negotiations alongside Jason Wilcox. The restructuring was designed to modernise operations and move away from manager-centric decision-making.
